In the early 1800s, a tiny border dispute birthed a micro‐nation. The 1783 Treaty of Paris left the Connecticut River’s source ambiguous. Three streams vied for the ‘northwestmost head,’ shifting the U.S.–Canada line. By 1832, 300 settlers declared independence[1s], drafting a constitution. Their government featured a bill of rights, three branches, and a militia. Both America and Britain taxed the land, sparking double‐ducks of levies. A British sheriff’s arrest ignited a skirmish[0.5s], drawing U.S. militia. By late 1835, U.S. troops occupied the area, ending the republic. The 1842 Webster‐Ashburton Treaty finally fixed the border, absorbing the land. Today, Pittsburg, NH, marks the spot where a forgotten nation once thrived[0.8s].
